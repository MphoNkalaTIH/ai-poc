MATHBOT: AI-Powered Holographic Math Education Robot
4th Year Engineering Design Project

Abstract 
Many learners struggle to understand mathematics due to limited access to interactive and engaging learning tools. Traditional teaching methods such as textbooks and standard digital platforms often present concepts in abstract ways, making them difficult for students to understand. This project proposes the design and development of Numina, an electronic educational robot that assists learners with mathematics through interactive visual demonstrations. The system will integrate embedded electronic hardware, microcontroller-based control, and a holographic LED display to present mathematical concepts in a more visual and engaging manner. The project focuses on the engineering design and integration of electronic subsystems including control circuitry, power management, and display control. By combining embedded electronics with interactive visualization, the proposed system aims to improve learner engagement while demonstrating the practical application of electronic engineering design principles. The outcome of the project will be a functional prototype capable of demonstrating mathematical concepts through an interactive electronic system

2. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND
2.1 Introduction 
Limited hands-on STEM learning opportunities remain a challenge in many schools and contribute to weaker understanding of subjects such as mathematics. STEM education encourages learners to develop problem-solving and analytical skills through hands-on engagement with scientific and technical concepts. However, many schools lack interactive tools that allow learners to explore these ideas in a practical way. Mathematics, which forms the foundation of many STEM fields, is especially affected when learners rely only on traditional teaching methods. Approaches such as textbooks, video tutorials, and basic educational software often do not provide sufficient interaction or adapt to different learning speeds. As a result, many learners struggle to understand mathematical concepts and lose interest in STEM subjects. Globally, only 58% of students achieve minimum mathematics proficiency [12]. In addition, many digital learning platforms show low long-term engagement, with retention rates of only 15–20% after 30 days (Statista, 2023). Access to STEM learning resources is also limited, with approximately 80% of schools lacking adequate STEM enrichment opportunities [13].
Although the broader challenge relates to limited hands-on STEM learning, this project focuses specifically on mathematics as a key area within STEM. Addressing this problem requires the design and integration of electronic hardware, embedded systems, and intelligent software to create an interactive learning platform. The development of such a system involves engineering decisions related to system architecture, sensing, user interaction, and control, making it a complex engineering problem. This project therefore aims to design an electronic educational system that supports more interactive and hands-on learning of mathematical concepts.
2.2 Background and Motivation
Many learners in South Africa experience mathematics anxiety and poor performance because concepts are often introduced too abstractly, without concrete examples. Over the past decade, mathematics performance has declined, and an increasing number of learners are opting for Mathematical Literacy instead of mathematics, limiting their future opportunities in STEM-related fields. Poor performance in mathematics also negatively affects learning in other subjects that rely on strong numerical and analytical skills, reducing the pool of students prepared for technical careers. Research in cognitive science shows that the Concrete-Representational-Abstract (CRA) approach can greatly improve understanding and retention of mathematical concepts [2]. However, most digital learning tools skip the concrete and representational steps, requiring learners to work with abstract ideas too early, which contributes to disengagement and poor comprehension.
Existing educational robotics platforms, such as LEGO Mindstorms and VEX, focus primarily on engineering concepts rather than mathematics learning. Mathematics tutoring applications like Khan Academy and Photomath lack hands-on interaction and physical feedback, which limits engagement. Although holographic projection technology has become more affordable, it has not been widely applied in education, and Edge AI solutions such as TensorFlow Lite and Raspberry Pi Zero 2W now allow for low-cost on-device inference without relying on cloud computing. Prior work in this area includes holographic displays used mainly in museum settings [4], conversational AI chatbots for education that lack physical embodiment, adaptive learning algorithms applied mostly to software platforms [1], and object recognition applied in laboratories but not for real-time learning of fractions or geometry[11].
Despite these advancements, there is currently no published solution that combines object recognition, holographic visualisation, voice AI, adaptive difficulty, and physical embodiment into a single integrated robotic educator. Providing hands-on, interactive learning tools can improve engagement, help learners understand abstract concepts through concrete experiences, and support better performance in mathematics, which is crucial for STEM participation and future career opportunities. This gap highlights the need for an engineering solution that integrates electronics, robotics, AI, and display technologies into an educational device that addresses the challenges of mathematics learning in South African schools.

3. ENGINEERING DESIGN METHODOLOGY
3.1 Design Strategy
The design of the proposed system follows a systems engineering approach, where the device is treated as an integrated system consisting of several interacting subsystems. The goal is to create an interactive electronic learning platform that combines sensing, intelligent processing, and visual feedback to support mathematics education.
The system consists of five main functional components:
User Interface: Allows learners to interact with the system through voice input, object interaction, and the mobile application.
Perception Module: Uses a camera sensor and computer vision algorithms to recognize objects and learning materials.
Processing Module: An embedded processing unit performs AI inference, mathematical reasoning, and system control.
Output Module: A fan-based holographic display and audio output provide visual and spoken explanations of mathematical concepts.
Personalisation Module: Adaptive learning software adjusts the difficulty and presentation of problems based on learner progress.

3.2 Alternative Solutions
Several alternative approaches were considered for developing an interactive mathematics learning tool.
Alternative 1: Software-Based Learning Application
A mobile or tablet-based application could provide AI tutoring and adaptive learning features.
Advantages:
Low development cost
Easy deployment
No additional hardware required
Disadvantages:
Limited hands-on interaction
No physical visualization of mathematical concepts
Similar to existing platforms such as Khan Academy
Decision: Rejected because it does not provide a unique interactive learning experience.
Alternative 2: Augmented Reality (AR) Learning System
An AR-based learning system using smart glasses or smartphones could display mathematical concepts in a 3D environment.
Advantages:
Highly immersive visual experience
Strong visual representation of mathematical concepts
Disadvantages:
Expensive hardware requirements
Potential user discomfort (motion sickness)
Limited accessibility for schools
Decision: Rejected due to high cost and limited practicality in classroom environments.
Selected Solution: Holographic AI Learning Device
The selected approach is a desk-based electronic learning device with a fan-based holographic display and AI interaction.
Advantages:
Provides engaging 3D visualization of mathematical concepts
Supports interactive learning through object recognition and voice interaction
Relatively low-cost hardware compared to AR solutions
Combines electronics, embedded systems, and AI into a single platform
This approach provides the best balance between educational effectiveness, technical feasibility, and engineering innovation.

Phase 2: Embedded Systems Design (Months 3-4)
Control System Architecture: Implement cascaded PID control loops for tracked base mobility:
Outer loop: Position/heading control (LiDAR-based SLAM)
Middle loop: Velocity control (encoder feedback)
Inner loop: Motor PWM (Hall effect sensor feedback)
Tuning via Ziegler-Nichols method; stability verified via Bode plots.
Software Stack:
ROS 2 (Humble distribution) for modular node design
Python 3.9 for core logic, C++ for real-time-critical tasks
TensorFlow Lite 2.10 for 97% smaller footprint than full TensorFlow
SymPy 1.11 for symbolic mathematics (fractions, equations, calculus)

Phase 3: AI/ML Development (Months 5-6)
Object Detection Model: Fine-tune MobileNetV2 (pre-trained on COCO dataset, 90 classes) on custom dataset:
Data collection: 500 images per object (pizza slices, bottles, geometric shapes, classroom items)
Augmentation: rotation, scaling, brightness adjustment (3× data multiplication)
Training: Google Colab TPU, 50 epochs, batch size 32
Expected performance: 85-90% mAP (good for edge deployment)

6. VERIFICATION AND TESTING STRATEGY
6.1 Hardware Testing Procedures
Unit Testing (Component-Level):
Power Supply: Verify Each DC-DC converter output within ±5% (multimeter)
Motors: Run at 50%, 75%, 100% PWM; verify torque (stall test); check thermal rise <50°C
Sensors:
RealSense: Depth accuracy within 2% (test with calibration grid)
LiDAR: 360° scan coverage; range accuracy ±5cm
Microphone: SNR ≥50dB (test with pink noise)
Projector: Lumens ≥100; colour gamut ✓; keystone correction works
Integration Testing (Subsystem-Level):
Perception Stack: Feed video → TensorFlow → check detection latency <100ms
Control Loop: Send heading command → verify response time <200ms via encoder feedback
Power Management: Run all systems simultaneously for 2 hours; measure battery drain (target: ≤12W avg)
Thermal: Operate at 25°C ambient for 4h; verify Jetson junction ≤80°C (margin to 85°C limit)
System-Level Testing (Full Integration):
Complete Workflow Test:
Show object to camera → detect → calculate → solve math → project hologram → speak answer
Measure end-to-end latency; log any failures
Stress Testing: 100 sequential interactions without restart; check for memory leaks
Safety Testing: Emergency stop button immediate cut-off; obstacle detection halts motion; no sharp edges
Acceptance Criteria:
≥95% successful interaction (1 failure allowed per 20 interactions)
Latency <500ms for 95% of interactions
No single point of failure causing data loss
Temperature within safe limits always
6.2 Software Testing
Unit Testing (Code-Level):
Test coverage ≥80% for critical functions (math solver, NLP, control loops)
SymPy solver verified against 100 known test cases (fractions, equations, graphs)
NLP entity extraction tested on 50 varied phrasings for each math concept
Integration Testing:
Mock hardware interfaces; verify firmware → math solver → output pipeline
Bluetooth communication protocol tested with simulated packet loss (5%, 10%)
Cloud API tested for race conditions (concurrent requests from multiple apps)
User Acceptance Testing (UAT):
5 internal testers (diverse math backgrounds) complete all learning levels
Checklist: Can students complete intended challenges? Do error messages make sense? Is UI intuitive?
6.3 Pilot User Testing (Validation)
Study Design:
Participants: 12-15 students (ages 9–14); mixed math proficiency (pre-screening)
Duration: 8 weeks; 3 sessions/week; 60 minutes/session
Control: Not applicable for prototype (no control group in final year project); compare pre/post only
Ethics: HREC approval required; parental consent; child assent; data minimisation
Assessment Instruments:
Pre-Assessment: Standardized math proficiency test (e.g., TIMSS sample items); self-reported confidence (1-10 scale)
Engagement Metrics: Session duration, return rate, interaction frequency (automated logging)
Learning Outcomes: Post-assessment (parallel form to pre-test); measure gain scores
Qualitative Feedback: Semi-structured interviews (30 min); "What did you like? What was hard? Would you use it again?"
Success Metrics:
Learning gain: ≥20% improvement (pre to post, p<0.05 paired t-test)
Engagement: ≥60% weekly return rate; sessions averaging ≥35 minutes
Usability: ≥7/10 System Usability Scale (SUS) score
Safety: Zero adverse events or injuries
Data Analysis:
Descriptive statistics (mean, SD, range)
Paired t-tests for pre/post comparisons
Qualitative coding of interview transcripts (thematic analysis)
Report results in academic paper format (5,000–8,000 words)
